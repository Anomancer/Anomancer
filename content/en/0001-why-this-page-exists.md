I’ve been following the development of AI for several years now, building my own systems, testing models and trying to understand what is actually happening around all of this.

Along the way, I’ve accumulated an absurd amount of information.

The problem is that a lot of it gets buried under technical jargon, marketing language and whatever the algorithms happen to amplify that day.

That is one of the reasons this page exists.

## Dispatches are a translation layer

The point is not to showcase every project I’ve built or document every line of code.

A much more useful question is:

**What can any of this actually do for a person?**

One article might look at why an AI agent should not be given unlimited permissions.

Another might be about how to recognize a weak claim.

Elsewhere I might write about algorithms, investing risk, automation at work, disinformation, language and thinking, or why metrics inside organizations can sometimes start steering the whole system in the wrong direction.

These subjects can look unrelated from the outside, but I often end up asking the same question:

**How does this system actually work, and what does it do to people?**

## You do not need to know the vocabulary first

There is a strange habit in technology discussions where people are expected to understand the terminology before they are allowed to understand the subject.

I think the order should be the opposite.

First understand the phenomenon.

Then give it a technical name if one is useful.

If AI agent permissions can be explained in ordinary language, I’ll use ordinary language. If something needs a diagram, I’ll make a diagram.

Complexity does not need to disappear.

It just does not need to be dropped on the reader all at once.

## Why now?

AI is no longer a small technology topic sitting in its own corner.

It affects work, media, education, software, economics, creative work and the way information is produced and distributed.

At the same time, the public conversation around it is heavily shaped by algorithms, commercial interests and constant competition for attention.

That creates a strange information environment.

In one direction, AI is apparently going to solve everything tomorrow.

In the other, the whole thing is just a bubble.

Reality is usually somewhere in the much more interesting space between those two.

That is the space I want to explore here.

## What is Anomancer?

**Anomancer is an entrance.**

It is one of several alter egos I use, but I chose this one for this role because the name describes what I do surprisingly well.

I’m interested in anomalies.

Places where something does not fit the expected model, behaves in an unexpected way or reveals something about a system that would otherwise remain hidden.

I’ve also spent quite a lot of my own life slightly outside the usual boxes.

Because of that, Anomancer is not just a name for technology or research.

There is another idea inside it:

**an anomaly is not automatically an error.**

Not everyone has had access to an environment, school, workplace or group of people that knew how to recognize their particular way of thinking, learning or creating as a strength.

Sometimes an ability stays invisible simply because it appears in the wrong place, or in the wrong form, compared with what the surrounding system knows how to reward.

Part of what I want to do with my work is push against that.

To show that useful things can come from unusual starting points, and that a real path does not have to resemble a ready-made career template.

If someone who has felt out of place, late, overlooked or simply wrong for the available boxes gets even a little more confidence in their own way of doing things from something I build, that matters to me.

Maybe that is why the name fits.

I study anomalies, build things around them, and I am one myself.

## Who am I?

I’ve followed AI closely since the first major wave of generative AI began to break into everyday use.

The last three years have been unusually intense.

Models, tools, interfaces and the entire conversation around them have sometimes changed in months, sometimes in weeks.

I’ve followed the global development closely, but also the way the same phenomenon appears in Finland.

One thing that interests me especially is the gap between what the technology can actually do and the way people talk about it.

A lot happens inside that gap.

There is uncertainty, exaggeration, dismissal, bad information and straightforward disinformation.

Algorithms make the whole thing even stranger because they tend to reward what produces a reaction, not necessarily what helps people understand what is happening.

These years have also been intense on a personal level.

I’ve spent a huge amount of time building, testing, reading, comparing models and trying to keep up with a field that does not wait around.

It has been extremely interesting.

It has also been difficult at times.

That is probably one reason I have become more interested in making all of this easier for other people to understand.

My original background is not in computer science or AI research.

I trained as a practical nurse and disability support worker.

I also trained as a ventilation installer.

Nothing particularly glamorous there.

There was no ready-made path from my education into AI systems, language models or software architecture.

I mostly ended up here by making things, studying things and connecting areas that do not necessarily look related on paper.

That probably also explains the way I look at technology.

I am rarely interested only in how a system works.

I am just as interested in what it does to people, what kinds of behavior it creates and what happens when a technical system collides with the real world.

## And the Observatory?

ANOMANCER is the entrance.

The Dispatches turn ideas into something a person can actually read and use.

If a subject starts to pull you deeper, the Observatory contains the other layers: systems, experiments, applications, research and the stranger machinery underneath.

You do not need to see all of it at once.

Finding one useful thing is enough.
